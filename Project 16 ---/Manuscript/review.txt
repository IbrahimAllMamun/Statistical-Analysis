Major concerns
1. The SLEDAI-2K category counts cannot give the reported mean. Table III gives a mean of 17.3 ± 13.3 (range 1–47), with 50% "low", 32% "moderate" and 18% "high". No cut-offs are stated. Using the conventional bands (mild 1–5, moderate 6–10, high ≥11), I calculated the highest mean the data could produce. Even if all 18 "high" patients scored the maximum of 47, the mean cannot exceed 14.1. Only a non-standard scheme such as ≤10 / 11–19 / ≥20 makes 17.3 possible, and then only if almost every patient scores at the top of their band. The authors must state their cut-offs and provide the score distribution (median, IQR, histogram).
2. "100% anti-dsDNA positive" contradicts a minimum SLEDAI-2K of 1. In SLEDAI-2K, raised DNA binding scores 2 points if the descriptor is present at the time of the visit or in the preceding 10 days. If every patient was anti-dsDNA positive at assessment, no patient could score below 2. So either the antibody results are historical, or the SLEDAI scoring left out the immunological items. Either way, the scoring is suspect.  (clinicaltrials)
3. The serology itself is implausible. Anti-dsDNA is found in roughly 60–70% of SLE patients, not 100%. Four patients are reported as ANA-negative but anti-dsDNA-positive, while HEp-2 IIF ANA at 1:80 has a pooled sensitivity of 97.8%. Three explanations are possible:  (tu-dresden)
anti-dsDNA positivity was effectively an entry requirement, which is a selection bias that must be disclosed;
a low-specificity ELISA produced false positives;
the data were transcribed wrongly.
The Methods paragraph listing three commercial ELISA thresholds is irrelevant to this study and should be deleted. The sentence stating that the assay "could not be independently verified from the available analytical records" is very concerning. It means the authors cannot vouch for their own laboratory data. The editor needs to know who holds the source data.
4. How was SLEDAI-2K scored without complement or urine data? Low complement scores 2 points, and casts, haematuria, proteinuria and pyuria score 4 each. C3/C4 and urinalysis are not reported anywhere, which matters especially with 46% lupus nephritis. The authors should state how each of the 24 descriptors was determined. They also say a "treating rheumatologist" scored the patients, yet recruitment was from Medicine, Nephrology and Dermatology. Who scored, and were they blinded to the questionnaires?
5. The adherence finding is confounded by newly diagnosed patients, probably fatally. 48% were newly diagnosed. Their SLEDAI-2K reflects untreated disease at presentation, so adherence cannot have caused it. MMAS-8 also asks about recent medication-taking behaviour, which is meaningless days after a first prescription. It is also striking that exactly 48% were newly diagnosed and exactly 48% had low adherence. I request:
a cross-tabulation of adherence category by diagnosis status;
the main analysis repeated in established-disease patients only, or with new-diagnosis status as a covariate (an interaction term would be better).
In addition, cyclophosphamide (25%) is given intravenously by hospital staff, so self-reported adherence does not apply to it. Did MMAS-8 cover it?
6. Unadjusted confounding and overlapping measures. The model leaves out age, sex, disease duration, new-diagnosis status, prednisolone dose (a known driver of both mood and fatigue), neuropsychiatric lupus and organ involvement. Meanwhile it includes haemoglobin and CRP, which have no clear rationale (CRP is notoriously unreliable in SLE). PHQ-9 contains fatigue, sleep and appetite items, so PHQ-9 and FSS partly measure the same thing. Required additions:
VIFs to check collinearity, plus R² and adjusted R²;
residual diagnostics;
Spearman correlations or a robust or transformed model, because SLEDAI is skewed (SD 13.3 against a mean of 17.3, minimum 1) and CRP (8.1 ± 13.0) cannot be summarised as a mean ± SD.
7. The effect sizes are out of line with the literature. An r of 0.736 between PHQ-9 and SLEDAI-2K would be among the strongest ever reported. The authors' own reference 6 (Liao 2022) is the best comparison: it studied 325 patients with PHQ-9 and reported no correlation coefficient. Its SLEDAI-2K cut-off for predicting depression was 8.5, with an AUC of only 0.660, which is a modest association. The authors' reference 20 (Mertz) also contradicts them: the PGA (p=0.004) but not the SELENA-SLEDAI (p=0.43) was significantly associated with fatigue. A Saudi outpatient study (Lupus 2018;27:327–332) found that disease activity did not correlate with medication non-adherence. The Discussion has to address why these results are so much stronger. Without good reasons, extremely tidy, strong correlations should make the editor more suspicious, not less.  (unica)  (nih)
8. Descriptive data and the depression labelling. Means and SDs of MMAS-8, PHQ-9 and FSS are not reported at all and must be added. The Abstract and Discussion say 43% had "severe" depressive symptoms, but severe (PHQ-9 ≥20) was 21%. The 43% is moderately severe plus severe. This misstatement must be corrected.
9. Questionnaire language, validation and licensing.
PHQ-9: a validated Bangla version exists (Naher et al., DUJBS 2021, 321 Bangladeshi adults). The authors should use and cite it.
FSS and MMAS-8: the authors must say which Bangla versions were used and how they were validated.
MMAS-8 licence: two journals retracted papers this year for unauthorized use of the scale. In June 2025, a judge ruled that neither the MMAS-4 nor the MMAS-8 is entitled to copyright protection, but retractions continue. The 2008 MMAS-8 validation paper was itself retracted in 2023 after flaws with the reported sensitivity and specificity were identified. The authors should state whether they held a licence, and the journal should decide its position.  (papers continue to face retractions for failure to license pricy tool +2)
10. Ethics, consent and provenance.
Ethical approval is dated February 2020, but data were collected in 2022. Was there an amendment or extension?
The memo number ("CMC/PG/…") suggests a postgraduate thesis. If so, this must be disclosed and checked for duplicate publication.
The age range is 13–50. Patients aged 13–17 need guardian consent plus assent, and PHQ-9 is not the adolescent instrument (PHQ-A).
No author is affiliated with Chattogram, and the text relies on "original study records". An ICMJE contributorship statement is needed, along with confirmation of who collected the data.
11. Classification criteria. Using ACR 1997 in 2022 needs justification, since SLICC-2012 and EULAR/ACR-2019 were available. In the validation cohort, the new criteria had a sensitivity of 96.1% and specificity of 93.4%, compared with 82.8% sensitivity for ACR 1997.
